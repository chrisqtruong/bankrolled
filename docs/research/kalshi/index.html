Following the money: Kalshi

In about 18 months Kalshi went from a niche federally licensed exchange to a $22 billion business built mostly on sports bets. This project traces where its money came from, how it makes money, and what it spends to win over regulators, politicians and young customers.

How this was made: researched and written with Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, which gathered public records and reporting, drafted the text and drew the charts. Chris Truong chose the subject, directed the work and reviewed it before publishing. Every figure links to its source so you can check it yourself.

The money map

Money in

Origins and investors

Kalshi came from Wall Street's quant side, not from gambling. Founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara, MIT graduates who worked at Citadel Securities, Bridgewater and Goldman Sachs, came up with the idea in 2018 while interning at the trading firm Five Rings, then joined Y Combinator in 2019 (Contrary, Forbes).

The license was the business. On November 4, 2020, the CFTC approved Kalshi as the first federally regulated exchange for event contracts (CFTC). That federal license is now Kalshi's shield against state gambling laws.

Who backs and steers it

  • Investors: Sequoia (since the 2021 Series A), Paradigm, Andreessen Horowitz, Coatue, Alphabet's CapitalG, Coinbase Ventures, Morgan Stanley and ARK Invest, among others.
  • Board: five members, including Sequoia's Alfred Lin (Reuters).
  • Brian Quintenz: a former CFTC commissioner on Kalshi's board, nominated to chair the CFTC in 2025. The nomination was withdrawn, and in July 2026 he became an adviser to the industry's lobbying coalition (SBC Americas).
  • Donald Trump Jr.: a paid strategic adviser since January 2025. Reported The Financial Times, citing unnamed sources, reported he was paid about $300,000 in Kalshi stock for the role; neither he nor Kalshi commented (Covers). His venture firm has also invested in rival Polymarket.

Money made

Fees, revenue, volume

Kalshi takes a fee on almost every trade, largest on near coin-flip events. It is private, so every revenue figure is an estimate or the company's own unaudited claim.

  • Growth: about $40.1 billion was traded on Kalshi in July 2026 alone, during the World Cup (The Block).
  • Reported Revenue pace: The Information, citing people familiar, reported a pace of about $4 billion a year by July 2026, with June operating costs near $300 million, mostly marketing (via Dealroom).
  • Traders mostly lose: economists at University College Dublin studied about 314,000 Kalshi contract prices and found traders lost about 20% on average before fees; buyers of long shots priced at 10 cents or less lost more than 60% (Burgi, Deng and Whelan).
  • Distribution: Robinhood (March 2025), Webull and Coinbase (all 50 states, January 2026) sell Kalshi contracts inside apps people already use. CNN, CNBC and Fox News show its odds on air, and the NHL, the US Open and five MLB teams are partners (Fortune).

Money for influence

Lobbying and politics

Who it hired

  • Former regulators: De'Ana Dow, once counsel to two CFTC chairmen, has lobbied for Kalshi since 2022. Former CFTC official Jeff Bandman now runs Kalshi Prime, its futures broker (Kalshi).
  • The CFTC's overseers: former Sen. Blanche Lincoln, who chaired the Senate Agriculture Committee, which oversees the CFTC.
  • Both parties' insiders: two former legislative directors to Chuck Schumer; a former Senate Republican floor staffer; the finance chair of Trump's second inaugural committee, through Miller Strategies; and a former Biden administration liaison, John Bivona, as head of federal relations.
  • The states: registered lobbyists in 41 states by September 2026, nearly all hired since April (OpenSecrets).
  • The trade group: the Coalition for Prediction Markets (Kalshi, Coinbase, Robinhood, Crypto.com, Underdog) is led by former Rep. Sean Patrick Maloney (Axios).

Political giving

The founders gave about $1 million to candidates and party committees in 2025 and 2026, roughly $615,000 to Republicans and $377,000 to Democrats, including $250,000 each to NRSC Victory in April 2026 (NOTUS). Recipients include sponsors of bills to restrict prediction markets as well as sponsors of the Kalshi-backed Prediction Market Act. Kalshi has no PAC of its own.

The opposition spends too. The American Gaming Association spent about as much on federal lobbying as Kalshi in the first half of 2026, and a super PAC funded by FanDuel, DraftKings and Fanatics has raised $70 million (NOTUS). Sportsbooks see Kalshi as a competitor.

Money for attention

Ads and who they reach
  • Stars: Timothée Chalamet spots in June 2026, and a World Cup sponsorship of Argentina's national team that included a paid Instagram post by Lionel Messi (Gambling Insider).
  • Influencers: in June 2026 the BBB's National Advertising Division referred Kalshi to the FTC and state attorneys general after Kalshi declined to take part in a review of paid posts that did not disclose the relationship (MediaPost).
  • Young customers: Kalshi lets 18-year-olds trade; most state sportsbooks require bettors to be 21. Creators pitched it as a "money hack," and a 2025 college ambassador program was deleted after backlash. Ohio cited that framing and underage access in a proposed $5 million fine (Covers).
  • Reported CNN estimated that 18- to 20-year-olds traded about $5.4 billion on Kalshi in 2026; Kalshi says the age group is about 3% of volume. CNN did not publish its method and is a Kalshi data partner (CNN via KEYT).

The legal fight

States, courts, the CFTC

Kalshi's whole model rests on one argument: its contracts are federally regulated swaps, so state gambling laws do not apply. The CFTC fought Kalshi until May 2025, when it dropped its appeal under the new administration (Finance Magnates). Since April 2026 the CFTC and the Justice Department have sued states on Kalshi's side of the argument (CFTC).

New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to settle the split on September 2, 2026 (No. 26-299). Kalshi's response is due November 9.

Maryland

Maryland ordered Kalshi to stop in April 2025. A federal judge refused to block the state's law that August, but Maryland agreed not to enforce it until the Fourth Circuit rules, so Kalshi still operates in Maryland (Covers). In April 2026 Gov. Wes Moore barred state employees from trading on inside information (Baltimore Banner), and in August Baltimore City sued Kalshi, Robinhood, Webull and Coinbase under its consumer protection law (The Block). The lawsuit's claims are allegations.

Other cases

Arizona filed criminal charges, which a federal judge blocked at the CFTC's request. New York's lawsuit seeks about $36 billion. Gamblers have filed class actions and suits to recover losses; none has produced a ruling against Kalshi yet (Front Office Sports). Kalshi says it has opened about 200 insider-trading investigations of its own users (NPR).

What the research finds

No study yet measures harm among prediction-market users directly. Because sports make up most of Kalshi's business, the research on online sports betting is the closest evidence.

StudyFinding
Hollenbeck, Larsen and Proserpio, Management Science (peer-reviewed)Online sports betting lowered credit scores; an earlier version of the study found bankruptcy risk up 25–30%.
Baker and colleagues, Journal of Financial Economics, 2026 (peer-reviewed)Betting cut savings and investment; households already short of money took on more credit card debt and overdrafts.
Ozer, Greenwood and Gopal, Information Systems Research, 2026 (peer-reviewed)Legal online betting raised calls to the problem-gambling helpline and was linked to more suicides among young, unmarried men.
Ayers and colleagues, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2025 (peer-reviewed)Searches for help with gambling addiction rose 23% nationally after 2018.
Arnesen and Matsuzawa, 2025 (working paper)Domestic violence after home-team NFL upsets rose roughly 9–10% where betting is legal.

A 2026 policy article in Science argues that prediction markets use gambling-style design while calling bets "trading," which keeps users from seeing themselves as gamblers (Gambling Insider). Kalshi is not connected to the state self-exclusion lists that licensed sportsbooks must honor. In May 2026 it pledged $2 million to the National Council on Problem Gambling (Covers).

The other side: Federal Reserve economists found Kalshi's markets give a useful, continuously updated read on what people expect from the Fed and the economy (NBER). Kalshi argues it is federally supervised, does not bet against its users and polices insider trading. Its forecasting value comes mostly from economic and political markets, though, while most of its money comes from sports.
